# Questioning for Learning: A Comprehensive Research Report

**Date**: 2026-03-07
**Prepared by**: Research Specialist Agent (Cornelius v03.26)
**Scope**: Teacher questioning, student-generated questions, question-driven pedagogy, cognitive science of questioning
**Target audience**: Upper secondary teacher in samhällskunskap (civics/social studies) and historia (history)
**Sources consulted**: 25+ peer-reviewed articles, systematic reviews, and institutional reports

---

## Executive Summary

Questioning is one of the most powerful and most misused tools in teaching. Research consistently shows that teachers ask too many low-cognitive-demand questions, give too little wait time, and rely on the Initiate-Response-Evaluate (IRE) pattern that limits genuine thinking. Studies indicate teachers spend 35-50% of instructional time on questioning, yet 60-80% of those questions target only recall [1]. The gap between what questioning could accomplish and what it typically does is enormous.

The evidence base has grown significantly since 2020. Key findings include: (1) extended wait time of 3-5 seconds after a question produces significantly richer student responses and more complex thinking [2]; (2) cold calling, when implemented well, closes gender participation gaps and improves learning without damaging classroom climate [3]; (3) elaborative interrogation - asking students "why does this make sense?" - is among the most powerful learning strategies identified by cognitive science [4]; (4) student-generated questions produce deeper learning than teacher-generated questions in many contexts [5]; and (5) dialogic teaching with open, exploratory questions produces measurable learning gains comparable to two additional months of schooling [6].

For Swedish upper secondary teachers in samhällskunskap and historia specifically, questioning around controversial issues presents both the highest stakes and the greatest opportunity. Skolinspektionen's 2022 inspection found that most Swedish students rarely participate in teacher-led discussions about controversial topics [7], while Skolforskningsinstitutet's systematic review on critical thinking confirms that teacher-guided questioning and discussion are the core mechanisms for developing genuine critical thinking [8]. The research offers clear, actionable guidance for closing this gap.

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: The Cognitive Case for Questioning - Why It Works

Questioning works because it forces active mental engagement rather than passive reception. Cognitive science identifies several mechanisms [4][9]:

**The testing effect / retrieval practice**: Being asked a question - even before learning - activates prior knowledge and creates memory traces that survive far longer than re-reading or re-listening. Questions that require retrieving information from memory strengthen that memory more than restudying.

**Elaborative interrogation**: When students are asked "why does this make sense?" or "why would this be true?" they are forced to connect new information to existing knowledge structures. This generates explanations that produce robust, transferable understanding. Multiple studies rate this as having "moderate utility" - more effective than highlighting, summarizing, or re-reading [4].

**Self-explanation effect**: When students explain their reasoning aloud or in writing - prompted by questions like "how did you get there?" or "explain your thinking" - they monitor their own comprehension and identify gaps. This metacognitive function is among the strongest single learning strategies.

**Desirable difficulties**: Questioning introduces a productive obstacle. Struggling to answer a hard question - even failing - produces better retention than studying without struggle. This is the cognitive science basis for Productive Failure approaches [10].

**Implication for practice**: Questions are not merely checks on what students already know - they are the primary mechanism by which knowledge becomes durable and transferable. Teachers should think of questioning as the engine of learning, not a diagnostic appendix.

### Finding 2: A Taxonomy of Question Types and Their Cognitive Demands

Research identifies a consistent hierarchy of question types mapped to Bloom's Revised Taxonomy [11][12]:

**Lower-order (recall and comprehension) - Levels 1-2:**
- "What happened in 1968?" / "Who was responsible for the Holocaust?"
- "What does 'imperialism' mean?"
- Role: Activate prior knowledge, check basic understanding, establish facts for further reasoning
- Cognitive demand: Low
- Appropriate use: Lesson openings, checking prerequisite knowledge before higher-order work

**Middle-order (application and analysis) - Levels 3-4:**
- "How does this example illustrate the concept of power?"
- "What pattern do you notice across these three sources?"
- "How do these two events connect?"
- Role: Build analytical habits, develop disciplinary thinking
- Cognitive demand: Moderate to high
- Appropriate use: Core of most lessons

**Higher-order (evaluation and creation) - Levels 5-6:**
- "To what extent was X the most important cause?"
- "Which perspective do you find most convincing, and why?"
- "How would you design a policy to address this problem?"
- Role: Develop judgment, synthesis, and original thinking
- Cognitive demand: High
- Appropriate use: Discussion, assessment, culminating tasks

**Key research finding**: Teachers' questions in secondary classrooms are dominated by lower-order questions [1][13]. A 2024 study of Chinese secondary biology classrooms found that most teacher questions required recall only, and students reported preferring higher-order questions despite their perceived difficulty [13]. Research consistently shows higher-order questions produce higher achievement and longer, more complex student responses [1].

**Important nuance**: Higher-order questions without the scaffolding of lower-order questions can fail. The research supports a sequential approach: establish facts, build connections, then ask for evaluation and synthesis. Bloom's levels are a ladder, not a ranking of moral worth.

### Finding 3: Wait Time - The Single Most Underutilized Technique

The systematic review of wait time research (2024, PMC) provides the most comprehensive recent synthesis [2]:

**Current reality**: Teachers typically wait **2.04 seconds** after asking a question (Wait Time 1) and **0.69 seconds** after a student response (Wait Time 2). These are extraordinarily brief pauses.

**Optimal duration**: Wait Time 1 and 2 should be extended to **3-5 seconds**. When teachers pause for 3+ seconds after a question:
- Students give longer, more detailed responses
- Voluntary participation increases
- Students produce more speculative and evaluative responses (higher-order)
- "I don't know" responses decrease
- The teacher asks fewer but higher-quality follow-up questions

**After student responses (Wait Time 2)**: This pause is even more neglected (0.69 seconds average). Extending it to 2-3 seconds signals that student thinking is valued and invites other students to engage.

**Diminishing returns**: One study found diminishing returns beyond 5 seconds for basic cognitive questions, with student attention dropping. The 3-5 second range appears optimal for most purposes.

**Low-cost training**: Simple interventions - video modeling, pamphlets, feedback - can reliably train teachers to extend wait time. It is one of the cheapest high-impact interventions available.

**Practical technique**: Announce wait time explicitly: "I'm going to give you 30 seconds to think before anyone answers." This externalizes the pause and signals that thinking, not speed, is valued.

### Finding 4: Cold Calling and Participation Equity

**What cold calling is**: Selecting a student to answer regardless of whether they have raised their hand. Sometimes called "no hands up."

**The participation problem cold calling solves**: In typical classrooms with voluntary hand-raising, participation is dominated by a small subset of students - typically those who already know the answer. This creates a systematic inequity and reduces cognitive engagement for everyone else.

**Gender equity evidence**: A landmark study found that when cold calling was used frequently:
- Women answered significantly more questions than in low-cold-calling conditions
- Gender participation gaps closed substantially
- Student comfort and satisfaction were not reduced [3]

**The anxiety concern**: Research does find some increased anxiety with cold calling, particularly for anxious students (Cooper et al., 2018). However, design matters enormously. Cold calling that follows adequate think time, that is normalized as an expectation (not a punishment), and that responds constructively to wrong answers produces different outcomes than ad hoc cold calling.

**Doug Lemov's approach (Teach Like a Champion 3.0)**: Cold calling works best when:
1. Question is asked BEFORE a student is nominated (so all students think)
2. There is adequate think time before the nomination
3. It is a normalized classroom expectation, not a random event
4. Wrong answers are treated productively, not punitively [14]

**Think-Pair-Share as a hybrid**: A 2024 study confirmed that TPS promotes more equitable participation and higher-quality discussion than whole-class questioning alone. Previously quiet students speak more; previously dominant students develop more nuanced responses. The think and pair steps are particularly well-supported; the "share" step's value is somewhat less certain [15].

**No-Opt-Out**: Lemov's technique of not accepting "I don't know" as a final answer - instead providing scaffolding until the student can contribute something - is consistent with a high-expectations classroom culture. Research supports the underlying principle: allowing opt-outs signals low expectations [14].

### Finding 5: Dialogic Teaching - The Bigger Frame

Robin Alexander's model of Dialogic Teaching provides a research-backed alternative to the dominant IRE (Initiate-Response-Evaluate) pattern [6]:

**The IRE problem**: In IRE, the teacher asks, a student answers, the teacher evaluates ("good," "correct," "close"). This pattern:
- Positions the teacher as sole arbiter of correctness
- Produces brief, tentative student responses
- Discourages risk-taking
- Fails to develop reasoning and argumentation skills
- Research confirms it is still the dominant pattern in secondary classrooms [1]

**Dialogic alternatives**: Alexander identifies questioning moves that open up rather than close down:
- **Uptake**: Taking a student answer and asking another student to respond to it ("What does everyone think about what Maria said?")
- **Press**: Asking for elaboration ("Can you say more about that?")
- **Revoicing**: Restating a student idea in clearer form ("So you're saying that...?")
- **Challenge**: Asking for evidence or counter-arguments ("What would someone who disagreed with you say?")
- **Building**: Explicitly connecting ideas across students ("How does what Jonas said connect to what Fatima said earlier?")

**Evidence base**: The 2017 EEF randomized trial of dialogic teaching with 5,000 pupils found an average of **+2 months** additional progress in English, mathematics, and science compared to control groups [6]. The intervention involved training teachers in these specific questioning techniques.

**Key insight**: Dialogic teaching is not simply "more discussion." It requires deliberate questioning design - choosing open questions, anticipating student responses, planning how to extend and challenge thinking.

### Finding 6: Student-Generated Questions as a Learning Strategy

Research consistently shows that students who generate their own questions learn more deeply than students who only answer teacher-generated questions [5][16]:

**Why it works**:
- Question generation requires deep processing: students must identify what they don't know
- It activates metacognition - students monitor their own understanding
- It creates ownership and intrinsic motivation
- Questions as goals are more motivating than facts as goals

**Effect on learning**: A literature review found that student-generated question activities had positive effects on exam achievement for both high- and low-performing students. Low-performing students benefited the most from student-generated questioning on comprehension measures [5].

**The Question Formulation Technique (QFT)**: Developed by Dan Rothstein and Luz Santana (Right Question Institute), used in over 1 million classrooms globally. The structured protocol:
1. Teacher provides a "Question Focus" (a statement or image, not a question)
2. Students generate as many questions as possible without stopping to answer, judge, or discuss
3. Students work in small groups, distinguishing open from closed questions
4. Students prioritize their three most important questions
5. Class reflects on what they learned by formulating questions

**Research on QFT**: Studies with the QFT show significant improvements in student question quality when taught by classroom teachers. Young children's questioning improves markedly. The technique is particularly suited to history and social studies, where questions can be attached to primary sources or historical images [16].

**Practical application for samhällskunskap and historia**:
- Use a political cartoon or news photograph as a Question Focus
- Use a primary historical source as a Question Focus
- Use a controversial statement as a Question Focus
- Students generate questions, then select the most important one to investigate

### Finding 7: Elaborative Interrogation - The High-Value Questioning Move

Elaborative interrogation (EI) is a specific questioning strategy with strong cognitive science support [4][17]:

**Definition**: Asking students to explain "why" a fact is true, or "why does this make sense?" rather than simply asking them to recall the fact.

**Example contrast**:
- Retrieval question: "What was the main cause of World War I?"
- Elaborative interrogation: "Why does it make sense that nationalism would have been a major factor in triggering World War I? What connections do you see?"

**Why it works**: EI forces students to connect new information to prior knowledge, generating explanations rather than simply retrieving facts. This produces richer encoding and more transferable knowledge.

**Research evidence**: Multiple studies rate EI as having "moderate utility" - significantly more effective than common strategies like rereading, highlighting, or summarizing [4]. A 2025 study specifically examined EI in academic reading and found positive effects on learning outcomes.

**Classroom implementation (2024 chapter)**: A recently published chapter on using EI in classroom settings recommends:
- Use EI primarily after students have basic familiarity with content (not as introduction)
- EI works better for factual content than for complex skills
- Written EI prompts (students write explanations) tend to be more effective than oral
- Short, focused EI prompts outperform long, complex ones

**Connection to samhällskunskap and historia**:
- "Why does it make sense that economic inequality often precedes political instability?"
- "Why would a primary source from this period likely be biased in this way?"
- "Why does the concept of demokratiskt deltagande matter to how we understand this election?"

### Finding 8: Questioning Wrong Answers - Error Climate

How teachers respond to wrong answers shapes classroom culture, risk-taking, and learning [18][19]:

**The challenge**: Wrong answers are information - they reveal student thinking and provide opportunities for learning. But many teachers respond in ways that shut down further risk-taking.

**Research on error climate**: Longitudinal research (2025, British Journal of Educational Psychology) shows that positive error climate - where errors are treated as learning opportunities - is associated with:
- Higher achievement
- Less alienation from teachers
- Greater motivation to try challenging tasks [19]

**Common teacher responses to wrong answers (research-documented)**:
- Quickly moving to another student (most common, least effective)
- Saying "not quite" or "close" without follow-up
- Overcorrecting without involving the student in the correction

**High-quality responses to wrong answers** (from 2023 Frontiers research on teacher "oops club" classrooms [18]):
- Pause 5 seconds to allow self-correction
- Ask "What makes you say that?" (treats the wrong answer as evidence of reasoning, not failure)
- Scaffold toward the correct answer: "You're right that X - what about Y?"
- Separate the person from the answer: "That's an interesting way to think about it - let's test it against the evidence"
- Use the wrong answer as a class resource: "Several people thought X - let's figure out why that's tempting"

**Productive Failure**: Research on productive failure (Kapur, refined 2024) shows students who struggle with problems before instruction outperform those who receive instruction first, even though they fail during the struggle phase. The failure is not incidental - it is the mechanism [10].

### Finding 9: Questioning for Formative Assessment

Dylan Wiliam's work on embedded formative assessment positions questioning as the core mechanism for assessment-capable classrooms [20]:

**Questions as evidence collection**: Questions are only formative when the evidence they produce is used to adjust teaching or learning. A question that confirms "students know this" and then moves on is not formative - it's diagnostic. A question that reveals a misconception and leads the teacher to address it is formative.

**Key formative questioning moves**:
- Questions that reveal thinking, not just answers ("How did you arrive at that?")
- Questions that assess the class, not just individuals (hinge questions, show-me techniques)
- Questions that students can use for self-assessment ("Before I answer: how confident are you on a scale of 1-3?")

**Hinge questions**: A specific technique (Wiliam) - a diagnostic question designed so that wrong answers reveal specific misconceptions rather than generic "doesn't know." The teacher reads the pattern of responses and hinges the lesson accordingly.

**Digital tools supporting formative questioning**:
A 2025 comparative analysis of Mentimeter, Google Forms, Socrative, and Kahoot found [21]:
- **Mentimeter**: Best for anonymous responses, open word clouds, and live polls. Reduces social anxiety around answering. Particularly effective for controversial topics where students fear judgment.
- **Kahoot**: Best for retrieval practice and gamified review. Competitive element raises energy but may suppress willingness to show uncertainty.
- **Socrative**: Best for nuanced formative data - teachers see individual responses in real time.
- Research suggests digital tools increase participation and engagement, though effects on deep learning specifically are smaller [21]

**For samhällskunskap and historia**: Anonymous Mentimeter polls on controversial value questions allow teachers to visualize the full range of views without social pressure shaping responses. This can then feed discussion.

### Finding 10: Questioning Sequences - From Simple to Complex

Research on effective questioning sequencing supports what Vygotsky's zone of proximal development theory implies: questions should start at the edge of current understanding and push progressively further [1][22]:

**The scaffolded questioning sequence**:
1. **Activation questions** (What do you already know/think about X?): Activate prior knowledge and surface misconceptions
2. **Clarification questions** (What exactly do you mean by X?): Establish shared understanding of key concepts
3. **Evidence questions** (What evidence supports/challenges X?): Develop sourcing and corroboration skills
4. **Connection questions** (How does X connect to Y?): Build integrative thinking
5. **Challenge questions** (What would someone who disagreed with you say?): Develop argumentation
6. **Synthesis questions** (What do you now think about X?): Consolidate and integrate learning

**Questioning chains**: A 2023 study (Springer) analyzed how "questioning chains" - sequences of related questions - can either deepen or derail thinking. Effective chains follow student responses and extend them; ineffective chains ignore student responses and follow a predetermined path regardless [22].

**Key error - the pre-set fishing expedition**: Many teachers ask a sequence of questions where they already know the answer they want and keep asking until a student lands on it. Students quickly recognize this pattern ("guess what I'm thinking") and respond strategically rather than analytically. Research confirms this as among the most damaging questioning patterns [1][14].

## Questioning in Samhällskunskap and Historia: Subject-Specific Findings

### Critical Thinking and Source Criticism in Samhällskunskap

Skolforskningsinstitutet's systematic review on critical thinking in samhällskunskap is the most directly relevant Swedish evidence base [8]:

**Effective teacher questioning for critical thinking involves**:
- Active discussion leadership: teacher guides dialogue toward reflection, questioning, and consideration of perspectives students haven't raised
- Text-based questioning: asking students to identify and explain perspectives presented in a text challenges them to reflect on their own views
- Questions that enable students to apply their reasoning to new examples (transfer)
- Providing "stödstrukturer" (support structures) that are gradually removed as students gain competence

**Critical thinking defined in the Swedish curriculum context**: The ability to interpret, examine, and evaluate different types of information about social issues - analytically distinguishing perspectives, consequences, and connections, including the ability to question one's own assessments.

**Discussion formats for safety**: Small group and pair discussions provide psychological safety before whole-class discussion. The review finds this particularly important for students who are less confident or whose views may differ from the social norm [8].

### Controversial Issues in Swedish Classrooms

**Skolinspektionen's 2022 inspection** (30 schools, grades 8-9, samhällskunskap and biology) [7]:

Key findings directly relevant to questioning:
- Students "rarely participated in teacher-led discussions about potentially controversial topics"
- Most discussions occurred in whole-class settings where few students spoke
- Social norms and peer pressure inhibited participation - group conformity effects were documented
- Gender differences: boys often deflected with humor; girls were more serious but the resulting conflict reduced quality
- Only 1/3 of schools showed high-quality handling of controversial issues
- Many teachers avoided controversial topics out of fear of conflict

**Good practice identified**: Schools with high quality showed shared pedagogical approaches, collaborative planning, and normalized democratic working methods. The structure of the discussion - not just the content - was decisive.

**Implications for questioning design**:
- Anonymous digital questioning (Mentimeter) before discussion surfaces real distribution of views
- Small groups before whole class reduces social pressure effects
- Explicit discussion protocols (e.g., roles: devil's advocate, evidence-checker, summarizer) distribute responsibility
- Normalizing "I'm not sure yet" as a valid answer

**Research on Swedish samhällskunskap teachers and controversial issues (Tandfonline, 2024)**: Swedish social studies teachers navigate between two duties: conveying democratic values (normative) and facilitating critical inquiry (procedural). Research found teachers often lead by example, maintaining neutrality while presenting diverse perspectives, though this can suppress explicit value discussion [23].

### Questioning in Historia (History Education)

**Historical thinking questions**: The shift from traditional fact-oriented history to historical thinking pedagogy (historiskt tänkande) is directly tied to specific questioning moves [24]:

**Second-order concept questions** (the "disciplinary" questions of history):
- **Causation**: "What combination of factors most plausibly explains X?" / "Why is 'cause' a more complex concept in history than in physics?"
- **Consequence**: "Who was affected by X, and how differently?"
- **Change and continuity**: "What changed, what stayed the same, and why does that distinction matter?"
- **Evidence**: "What kind of source is this?" / "What can we learn from it and what can't we learn from it?"
- **Significance**: "Why should we remember X? Who decided it was significant?"
- **Perspective**: "How would a person living in this period have understood this event?" / "How does our perspective now differ from theirs?"

**A 2023 study on historical perspective-taking** found that students who regularly engaged with perspective-taking questions showed improved ability to understand historical actors' motivations and reduced presentism (judging the past by present standards) [24].

**Source criticism questions** (critical for samhällskunskap as well):
- "Who created this source, and why?"
- "What is this source trying to achieve?"
- "What might this source be concealing or distorting?"
- "How does this source compare to others from the same period?"
- "What questions can this source NOT answer?"

**Existentializing history**: Swedish research (Historieundervisningens existentialiserande potential) suggests that the most powerful questions in history are those that connect past and present in ways that matter to students' lives - questions of meaning, identity, responsibility, and justice [25]. This connects to the wider literature on authentic questioning: questions the teacher herself does not have a predetermined answer to.

## Trends and Patterns

### Trend 1: AI and Questioning

Recent research (2025) examines both opportunities and risks:
- AI chatbots (ChatGPT, etc.) can generate Bloom's-aligned questions at specified cognitive levels, with good performance at lower levels and improving performance at higher levels [26]
- Research on AI Chatbot-Assisted interactive learning environments (Springer, 2025) found that strategic teacher questioning in AI-augmented classrooms remained as important as in traditional settings [27]
- Concern: Students increasingly use AI to generate answers to teacher questions, potentially bypassing the cognitive work that questions are designed to produce [27]
- Opportunity: Teachers can use AI to rapidly generate diverse question sets, including higher-order questions, and then curate and sequence them

### Trend 2: Oracy and Questioning

The 2024 UK Oracy Education Commission (to which Robin Alexander submitted evidence) marks a wider recognition that learning to speak and question is a core educational outcome, not a delivery mechanism [6]. Questioning is increasingly positioned as developing students' capacity to question - a civic and epistemic competence - not merely a teaching technique.

### Trend 3: Equity in Questioning

Multiple 2024-2025 studies highlight systematic inequities in questioning:
- Cold calling benefits women more than men in participation terms [3]
- Students from disadvantaged backgrounds, and those who are introverted or have disabilities, are systematically underrepresented in voluntary participation [15]
- Structured discussion protocols, anonymous digital tools, and TPS increase participation equity

### Trend 4: Metacognition and Self-Questioning

Research on self-regulated learning (2024, EEF guidance) positions metacognitive questioning - teaching students to question their own understanding - as one of the highest-impact, lowest-cost interventions available (+7 months' gain estimate). Techniques include:
- "Before you start: what do you already know about this? What confuses you?"
- "While working: are you making progress? What's blocking you?"
- "After: what did you learn? What questions do you still have?"

## Common Mistakes Teachers Make with Questioning

Based on the research synthesis, the most common and most damaging teacher questioning errors are [1][13][14][28]:

1. **Too many closed, recall questions**: 60-80% of teacher questions target recall only. The fix: consciously design questions at multiple Bloom's levels before the lesson.

2. **Insufficient wait time**: Average wait is 2 seconds. The fix: externalize the pause ("30 seconds to think"), count silently, resist the urge to fill silence.

3. **The fishing expedition**: Asking a sequence of hints until a student lands on the predetermined answer. The fix: ask genuine questions where you value different possible answers.

4. **Only calling on volunteers**: Allows most students to opt out of thinking. The fix: cold calling after wait time, TPS, or structured turn-taking.

5. **Failing to follow up wrong answers**: Moving on after a wrong answer signals low expectations and misses learning opportunities. The fix: pause, probe, scaffold - "What makes you say that?"

6. **IRE dominance**: Teacher asks, student answers, teacher evaluates - no student-to-student dialogue. The fix: uptake, revoicing, challenge moves that create multi-directional discussion.

7. **Avoiding controversial questions**: Particularly in samhällskunskap and historia, teachers avoid the hardest questions that would most develop critical thinking. Skolinspektionen documents this systematically [7]. The fix: structured protocols, anonymous tools, small group before whole class.

8. **Not connecting questions to assessment criteria**: Questions feel arbitrary to students. The fix: make the connection explicit - "This question is the kind of reasoning required for the highest grade."

9. **Not teaching students to generate their own questions**: Student-generated questions are underused despite strong evidence for their effectiveness [5][16]. The fix: regular QFT-style activities, explicit instruction in question quality.

10. **Inconsistent error climate**: Praising some wrong answers as "good tries" while dismissing others. The fix: consistent, predictable response protocols for wrong answers.

## Recommendations for Samhällskunskap and Historia Teachers

**Immediate, low-effort changes:**

1. **Count to five silently after every question.** Put a sticky note on your desk reminding you. This single habit change produces measurable improvements in response quality.

2. **Change your response to "I don't know."** Instead of moving on, ask: "What do you think the answer might be?" or "What do you know that might be relevant?" Make participation non-optional but supportive.

3. **Ask "why does this make sense?" more than "what happened?"** Replace one recall question per lesson with an elaborative interrogation question.

4. **Use Mentimeter for the first five minutes on controversial topics.** Anonymous polling on "What do you think about X?" surfaces the real distribution of views before social conformity pressure shapes responses. Then discuss what you see.

**Medium-effort changes:**

5. **Learn and practice three dialogic moves.** Start with "uptake" (responding to student answers by asking others to build on them) and "press" ("can you say more about that?"). These fundamentally change the conversational structure.

6. **Run a QFT activity once a unit.** Present a primary source and ask students to generate questions. No answers allowed during generation. Then prioritize questions and investigate the most important ones.

7. **Plan your questioning sequence before the lesson.** Identify at least one activation question, one elaborative interrogation question, one evaluation question, and one synthesis question. Map them to your lesson structure.

8. **Design one hinge question per lesson** that reveals specific misconceptions. Use Mentimeter or show-of-hands to assess the full class, not just volunteers.

**Longer-term culture changes:**

9. **Build an error-positive classroom climate.** Celebrate wrong answers that reveal interesting thinking. Develop consistent language for responding to errors: "Let's figure out why that seems right even though it isn't."

10. **Make questioning itself an object of instruction.** Teach students Bloom's levels. Teach them to distinguish open from closed questions. Teach the QFT. Students who understand questioning as a skill become better askers and answerers.

11. **Normalize controversy in samhällskunskap.** Use structured discussion protocols (e.g., Socratic Seminar, philosophical chairs, fishbowl) to give students experience with teacher-facilitated disagreement. The Skolinspektionen data shows this is currently a significant gap in Swedish secondary education.

12. **Connect questioning to betygskriterier (grading criteria).** Make explicit to students that the kind of reasoning required by higher-grade descriptors - "nyanserat," "välgrundat" - is exactly the kind of reasoning higher-order questions develop.

## Challenges and Risks

- **Curriculum pressure and coverage**: High-order questioning takes more time per topic. Teachers must make peace with covering less, more deeply. Research consistently supports this trade-off.
- **Classroom climate fragility**: Cold calling and high-expectation questioning can backfire in classrooms without established trust. Culture-building precedes technique.
- **Teacher expertise requirement**: Dialogic teaching is cognitively demanding for teachers. It requires subject-matter depth to follow student thinking in unpredictable directions.
- **AI confounding**: Students using AI to generate or check answers undermines the cognitive work questioning is designed to produce. This is an emerging and unresolved challenge.
- **Peer pressure effects in Sweden**: Jantelagen (the Law of Jante) and group conformity pressures may dampen individual expression in discussions, particularly in whole-class settings. Anonymous tools and small-group structures partially address this.

---

## Conclusions

The research presents a clear picture: questioning is the most important and most underused lever for deep learning available to classroom teachers. The dominant pattern - rapid, low-cognitive-demand questions with minimal wait time, accepted by volunteers, followed by IRE evaluation - is the antithesis of what cognitive science recommends.

The good news is that the highest-impact changes require habit and design shifts, not resources. Extending wait time to five seconds costs nothing. Asking "why does this make sense?" instead of "what happened?" costs nothing. Designing a lesson with questions at multiple Bloom's levels takes 10 minutes.

For Swedish upper secondary teachers in samhällskunskap and historia specifically, the research points to a particular gap and a particular opportunity: controversial questions are currently underused, discussions are teacher-dominated, and students rarely generate their own questions. These are precisely the conditions under which the research shows the highest potential gains. The skills developed through well-structured controversial-issue discussion - argumentation, perspective-taking, evidence evaluation, civil disagreement - are the core competencies of democratic citizenship and the highest descriptors of samhällskunskapens kunskapskrav.

## Research Notes

- The field of teacher questioning is large and somewhat fragmented - findings from primary school may not transfer directly to upper secondary. Where possible, secondary-level evidence is prioritized.
- Much of the evidence base on questioning is correlational rather than experimental. The EEF dialogic teaching trial is a notable exception as a large randomized trial.
- Swedish-specific research on questioning strategies is limited. The Skolforskningsinstitutet systematic review on critical thinking in samhällskunskap is the strongest Swedish source, but focuses on the subject more broadly than questioning specifically.
- Skolinspektionen's 2022 report is a quality inspection rather than a research study, but its documentation of what is currently missing in Swedish classrooms is directly relevant.
- The AI and questioning literature is emerging rapidly. Findings from 2024-2025 on AI-augmented questioning contexts should be treated as preliminary.
- Effect sizes specific to teacher questioning as an isolated intervention are difficult to extract from the literature - questioning is typically studied as part of broader pedagogical approaches (dialogic teaching, formative assessment, etc.).
