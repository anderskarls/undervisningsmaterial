# Cognitive Mechanisms for Learning — Research Report 2026-04-23

*Research focus: cutting-edge 2024-2026 cognitive science applied to adolescent classroom learning (gymnasium, ages 16-19). Complements existing coverage of retrieval practice, pretesting, desirable difficulty, and rubric calibration by targeting adjacent mechanisms.*

## Executive Summary

The 2024-2026 cognitive-science literature converges on three pedagogically urgent findings. First, generative AI produces short-term performance gains but systematically damages the neural, metacognitive, and memory substrates that sustain long-term learning - the MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT" EEG study (Kosmyna et al., 2025) documents measurable reductions in brain connectivity, 83 percent inability to quote one's own AI-assisted essay, and a cross-over effect where prior LLM users remain under-engaged even when later asked to work unaided. Gerlich (2025, N=666) and Fan et al. (2024, BJET) corroborate this through behavioural and self-regulation measures, naming the phenomenon "cognitive debt" and "metacognitive laziness". The pedagogical implication for gymnasium is not a blanket ban but a careful sequencing: students must build schemas before offloading to AI, and AI support must be scaffolded with metacognitive prompts.

Second, the smartphone-in-classroom question has moved from debate to evidence. Abrahamsson's (2024) Norwegian natural experiment across 400+ middle schools shows that full-day bans produced a 60 percent reduction in girls' mental-health visits, 46 percent less bullying, improved GPAs, higher external-math scores, and an 8-14 percent increase in girls entering academic high-school tracks - with lower-SES students benefiting most. Effects on attention are replicated in the meta-analytic literature (mere-presence effects g=-0.14; brain-drain replications mixed but notification effects robust). The strongest signal: mere presence and notification availability degrade attention even without active use.

Third, the mechanism debates around cognitive load (element interactivity as the unifying variable; expertise reversal as its corollary), interleaving (fails for rule-based learning, low-similarity categories, and low-achieving adolescents), and encoding modality (handwriting produces broader theta/alpha EEG connectivity than typing) all push in the same pedagogical direction: initial learning needs structured, worked-example-heavy, hand-written, low-distraction conditions; transfer and retention benefit from carefully introduced desirable difficulties only after a minimum schema threshold is crossed. This "blocked-first-then-interleave" pattern is now empirically validated specifically for low-achieving adolescents (Hwang, 2025).

## 1. Cognitive Load Theory Updates

### Cognitive Load Theory: Emerging Trends and Innovations (Chen & Kalyuga, 2025)
- **Venue**: Education Sciences (MDPI), 15(4), 458
- **URL**: https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/4/458
- **Key findings**: All classic CLT effects (worked example, split-attention, modality, expertise reversal) are now argued to reduce to a single underlying construct: **element interactivity**. As expertise grows, previously separate elements chunk into a single element, and CLT effects shrink and can reverse. This provides a unified theoretical vocabulary rather than a catalogue of independent effects.
- **Novel mechanism**: Proposes that "germane load" be dropped from the tripartite model (intrinsic/extraneous/germane) because it cannot be measured independently of intrinsic load. Frames CLT as a 2-dimensional model: productive (intrinsic) load and unproductive (extraneous) load.
- **Pedagogical implication for gymnasium**: A worked example is effective for novices precisely because it reduces element interactivity; the same example becomes redundant and harmful for students who already have the schema. Teachers should diagnose prior knowledge (not age/grade) before choosing between worked examples, faded examples, and free problem-solving.

### A Cornerstone of Adaptivity - A Meta-Analysis of the Expertise Reversal Effect (Rey et al., 2025)
- **Venue**: Learning and Instruction, S0959475225000660
- **URL**: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660
- **Key findings**: Meta-analytic confirmation that expertise reversal is robust across domains and materials. Instructional scaffolding that boosts novice learning (worked examples, process prompts, integrated explanations) measurably *harms* learning in students with existing schemas. The effect is moderated by how finely expertise is measured - coarse proxies (grade, age) underestimate the effect.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Justifies fading support within a single lesson sequence. In samhällskunskap, begin a new concept (e.g., "ideology") with heavy scaffolding, but by lesson 3 shift to open inquiry; continued scaffolding becomes extraneous load for students who have the schema.

### Rethinking Pre-Training: Cognitive Load Implications for Learners with Varying Prior Knowledge (2025)
- **Venue**: Frontiers in Psychology, 16
- **URL**: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1628047/full
- **Key findings**: A contrarian finding - pre-training (priming key concepts before main instruction) reduced extraneous load across all learners, including those with high prior knowledge. This challenges the expected expertise reversal effect for pre-training specifically, suggesting that activating existing knowledge is different from providing redundant instruction.
- **Pedagogical implication**: A 2-3 minute "concept warm-up" at the start of a lesson (retrieve key terms, sketch a mini-schema) appears not to harm advanced students. Low-cost, low-risk practice.

### Cognitive Load Theory and Individual Differences (Seufert et al., 2024)
- **Venue**: Learning and Instruction, 91
- **URL**: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1041608024000165
- **Key findings**: Individual learning is more effective for high-element-interactivity materials presented in integrated format; collaborative learning in dyads is more beneficial when information is presented in dispersed format. Individual differences (working-memory capacity, prior knowledge, self-regulation) interact systematically with instructional design.
- **Pedagogical implication**: For complex integrated materials (e.g., a causal diagram of WW1 causes), let students study alone first; for dispersed materials (primary-source packet), pairs/dyads produce better learning.

## 2. Working Memory in Adolescents

### A Systematic Realist Review of School-Based Working Memory Training (Randall & Tyldesley, 2025)
- **Venue**: Zeitschrift für Psychologie, 233(2)
- **URL**: https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1027/2151-2604/a000596
- **Key findings**: Near-transfer effects (to WM tasks resembling training) are reliable and moderate; far-transfer to academic achievement remains weak and highly context-dependent. Implementation fidelity, teacher engagement, and alignment with curriculum content predict whether gains transfer. Adolescent-specific samples are underrepresented.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Direct WM training (apps like Cogmed) is a poor investment for gymnasium. Better: **reduce WM demands** of tasks via chunking, graphic organisers, and explicit schemas.

### Examining Working Memory Training for Healthy Adults - A Second-Order Meta-Analysis (Teixeira-Santos et al., 2024)
- **Venue**: Journal of Intelligence, 12(11), 114
- **URL**: https://www.mdpi.com/2079-3200/12/11/114
- **Key findings**: Second-order meta-analysis confirms small near-transfer, essentially zero far-transfer to fluid intelligence or academic tasks. The "WM training cures academic struggle" hypothesis is effectively dead.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Stop treating WM as a trainable muscle. Treat it as a fixed resource to be conserved through instructional design.

### Influence of SES, Working Memory and Academic Self-Concept on Academic Achievement in Adolescents (Nussbaumer et al., 2022, updated 2024 analyses cited in 2024-25 literature)
- **Venue**: European Journal of Psychology of Education
- **URL**: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10212-022-00599-9
- **Key findings**: In 2,379 middle/high-school adolescents, individual differences in WM (and to a lesser extent academic self-concept) account for most of the negative impact of low SES on academic achievement. WM is the proximal mechanism linking SES to outcomes.
- **Pedagogical implication**: For low-SES students in gymnasium, WM-conserving instruction (pre-print notes, partial worked examples, concept maps) is an equity intervention, not a remedial tool.

## 3. Attention, Mind-Wandering, Distraction

### Smartphone Bans, Student Outcomes and Mental Health (Abrahamsson, 2024)
- **Venue**: NHH Department of Economics, Discussion Paper No. 01
- **URL**: https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/3119200/DP%2001.pdf
- **Sample**: 400+ Norwegian middle schools, staggered ban introduction 2010-2019
- **Key quantitative findings**:
  - **~60 percent** reduction in healthcare visits for psychological symptoms among girls after full-day bans
  - **46 percent** reduction in girls' reported bullying
  - Improved GPAs and externally graded math exam scores (externally graded, not teacher-graded - rules out grade inflation)
  - **4-7 percentage point** increase in girls entering academic high-school tracks (8-14 percent relative increase)
  - Effects concentrated among girls and among students from lower-SES backgrounds
- **Pedagogical implication**: This is the strongest causal evidence yet for full-day phone restriction. Supports Sweden's 2024-2025 policy trajectory under Skolverket/utbildningsdepartementet.

### Distractions in Digital Reading: A Meta-Analysis of Attentional Interference Effects (2025)
- **Venue**: Frontiers in Psychology, 16
- **URL**: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1671214/full
- **Key findings**: Digital reading environments significantly impair comprehension when distractions are present. Internal distractions (mind-wandering) and external distractions (notifications, hyperlinks) both contribute; the combination is multiplicative, not additive.
- **Pedagogical implication**: When students must read long-form historical sources, prefer paper or e-readers (no notifications). Laptop/tablet reading for comprehension tasks is a last resort.

### Does the Mere Presence of a Smartphone Impact Cognitive Performance? A Meta-Analysis (Marty-Dugas et al., 2024)
- **Venue**: Media Psychology, 27(5)
- **URL**: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213269.2023.2286647
- **Key findings**: Meta-analytic effect of mere phone presence on cognitive performance: g=-0.14, significant but small. The original Ward et al. (2017) "brain drain" finding has had replication difficulties, but the overall meta-analytic signal is negative. Attention tasks show larger effects than memory tasks.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Phones in backpacks/lockers (not pockets/desks) is the operationalisation supported by data. Silent mode alone is insufficient.

### Beyond the Buzz: Effects of a Notification-Disabling Intervention (2024)
- **Venue**: Media Psychology
- **URL**: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2024.2334025
- **Key findings**: Disabling notifications (not just silencing) improves sustained attention and reduces task-switching costs. Users underestimate how much notifications fragment their attention even when they do not consciously check.
- **Pedagogical implication**: If gymnasium policy allows phones in bags, encourage students to disable all notifications for subjects requiring deep reading (long historical documents, research tasks).

### A Developmental Perspective on Mind Wandering (Alvarez et al., 2025)
- **Venue**: Developmental Review
- **URL**: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105381002500025X
- **Key findings**: Context-inappropriate mind-wandering is elevated in adolescence and correlates with poorer academic self-regulation, self-efficacy, and academic self-concept. Unlike earlier views treating mind-wandering as purely negative, developmental perspective distinguishes *goal-directed* daydreaming (potentially generative) from *task-interfering* wandering.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Short "planned wandering" breaks (2-3 min) between dense content blocks may be more effective than fighting mind-wandering continuously.

## 4. Multimedia Learning / Dual Coding

### The Past, Present, and Future of the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (Mayer, 2024)
- **Venue**: Educational Psychology Review
- **URL**: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09842-1
- **Key findings**: Mayer's own 2023-2024 retrospective consolidates 15+ principles into five foundational groups: reducing extraneous processing (coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial/temporal contiguity), managing essential processing (segmenting, pre-training, modality), fostering generative processing (multimedia, personalization, voice, image, embodiment), social/affective factors, and individual differences.
- **Pedagogical implication for gymnasium**: The "coherence principle" (remove decorative, tangential content) is the highest-leverage intervention for slide-heavy teaching in samhällskunskap. Most PowerPoints violate it.

### Supporting Learning with AI-Generated Images (MIT Sloan Teaching & Learning Technologies, 2024)
- **URL**: https://mitsloanedtech.mit.edu/2024/03/06/supporting-learning-with-ai-generated-images-a-research-backed-guide/
- **Key findings**: AI-generated images are only learning-effective when they are *instructive* (directly supporting target concept). Decorative AI images, even when "relevant-ish", increase extraneous load and reduce learning (coherence violation). The ease of generation tempts teachers to over-produce visuals.
- **Pedagogical implication**: When using nano-banana or similar, ask: does this image contain information students must encode? If no, omit it.

### Higdon, Neath, Surprenant & Ensor (2025) on Picture Superiority
- **Context**: Challenges dual-coding as the mechanism for the picture-superiority effect; argues distinctiveness is the primary driver.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Pictures help when they are *distinctive relative to surrounding verbal content*, not because they add a second code per se. Implication: a single striking image per slide outperforms three generic ones.

## 5. Cognitive Effects of Generative AI on Learning

**This section is the most pedagogically urgent in 2026.** The 2024-2026 evidence reveals a consistent pattern: short-term task-performance gains (moderate-to-large effect sizes) coexist with long-term learning deficits measurable at neural, behavioural, and metacognitive levels.

### Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt (Kosmyna, Hauptmann, Yuan, Situ, Liao, Beresnitzky, Braunstein & Maes, 2025, MIT Media Lab)
- **Venue**: arXiv:2506.08872
- **URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
- **Design**: 54 participants in 3 conditions (LLM, Search Engine, Brain-only), three essay-writing sessions; 18 crossed over in Session 4. EEG recording of brain connectivity.
- **Key quantitative findings**:
  - **Up to 55 percent reduced neural connectivity** in LLM group vs. Brain-only
  - Weaker alpha and theta brain waves in LLM users, consistent with bypassing deep memory processes
  - **83 percent of LLM users could not quote anything from their own just-written essay**
  - Lowest self-reported essay ownership in LLM group
  - **Cross-over effect**: Participants who used LLM for 3 sessions and then wrote without it in Session 4 still showed reduced alpha/beta connectivity - **cognitive under-engagement persisted after tool removal**
- **Novel concept**: "Cognitive debt" - the idea that AI assistance spares mental effort in the short term at the cost of weakened critical thinking, creativity, and memory integration long-term. The term frames the phenomenon as accumulated over time, not a one-shot effect.
- **Pedagogical implication**: The cross-over finding is the most alarming. It suggests that habitual AI use during essay drafting damages the student's independent writing process even when they are told to work without AI. For Swedish gymnasium, this validates Skolverket's cautious stance and supports AI-free initial drafting, AI-assisted revision.

### AI Tools in Society: Impacts on Cognitive Offloading and the Future of Critical Thinking (Gerlich, 2025)
- **Venue**: Societies, 15(1), 6
- **URL**: https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6
- **Sample**: 666 participants, mixed-methods (ANOVA + thematic interviews)
- **Key findings**: Significant **negative correlation** between frequency of AI tool use and critical-thinking scores, mediated by cognitive offloading. Younger participants (17-25) showed the highest AI dependence and lowest critical-thinking scores - directly relevant to gymnasium age group.
- **Pedagogical implication**: The age-gradient finding is crucial. Gymnasium students are the most vulnerable demographic because they are forming epistemic habits while having highest AI access.

### Beware of Metacognitive Laziness (Fan, Tang, et al., 2024/2025)
- **Venue**: British Journal of Educational Technology, 56
- **URL**: https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjet.13544
- **Design**: Randomized experiment, four conditions (ChatGPT, human expert, writing analytics tools, no support), measuring self-regulated-learning (SRL) process traces.
- **Key findings**:
  - ChatGPT group produced **highest essay scores** on the immediate task
  - But **no significant difference in knowledge gain or transfer** to new tasks
  - SRL process analysis showed ChatGPT users skipped evaluation, monitoring, and orientation stages - they simply executed LLM suggestions
  - Intrinsic motivation was not boosted by ChatGPT use
- **Novel concept**: "Metacognitive laziness" - learners delegate the regulatory processes of learning, not just the execution. The regulation *is* the learning.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Grading students on final essay quality masks the underlying learning deficit. Process assessment (drafts, think-alouds, oral defense) becomes critical.

### The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking (Lee, Sarkar, et al., 2025 - Microsoft/CMU)
- **Venue**: CHI 2025 Proceedings
- **URL**: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713778
- **Design**: Survey of 319 knowledge workers (N=936 AI-assisted tasks), self-reported cognitive effort and confidence.
- **Key findings**: Users with **higher confidence in AI** engaged in less critical thinking; users with **higher self-confidence** engaged in more. AI use shifts cognitive effort from *task execution* to *response verification*. The former involves generating, the latter evaluating.
- **Pedagogical implication**: This is not purely negative - evaluating AI output is itself a skill. But it replaces generative thinking with evaluative thinking, which are different cognitive operations. Students need explicit instruction in verification skills.

### Effects of GenAI on K-12 and Higher Education Learning Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis (Liu, Guo, He & Hu, 2025)
- **Venue**: Journal of Educational Computing Research
- **URL**: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/07356331251329185
- **Key findings**: Combined effect size of GenAI on learning outcomes: **g ≈ 0.68** (moderate-large positive) for immediate task outcomes. Moderator analyses show **stronger effects with teacher support** (g=1.43 with support vs. g=0.08 without). Effects shrink for longer-term measures.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Unguided AI use is essentially ineffective; teacher-scaffolded AI use is highly effective. The default student behaviour (unguided ChatGPT use) captures none of the potential benefit.

### Does GenAI Improve Students' Higher-Order Thinking? A Meta-Analysis of 29 Experiments (2025)
- **Venue**: Journal of Intelligence, 13(12), 160
- **URL**: https://www.mdpi.com/2079-3200/13/12/160
- **Key findings**: Moderate positive effect on higher-order thinking overall, **strongest for problem-solving**, smaller for **critical thinking**, weakest for **creativity**. GenAI's impact on creativity is essentially limited.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Treat AI as a problem-solving aid, not a creativity tool. In samhällskunskap, use AI for structured analysis tasks, not open-ended essay ideation.

## 6. Prior Knowledge & Schema Building

### Leveraging What Students Know: Prior Knowledge Activation (Hattan, Alexander & Lupo, 2024)
- **Venue**: Review of Educational Research
- **URL**: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543221148478
- **Key findings**: Comprehensive review of prior-knowledge-activation (PKA) techniques. **Retrieval-based PKA** (ask students what they know before new content) outperforms **provision-based PKA** (tell them the relevant prior concepts). Effect is larger for students with less prior knowledge.
- **Pedagogical implication**: The 11th-grade heart-electrical-system study cited in the review showed stronger consolidation after students recalled their existing knowledge before new instruction - especially students starting with *less* prior knowledge. Activating even weak prior schemas builds encoding hooks.

### Rethinking Pre-Training (Frontiers in Psychology, 2025)
- See Section 1 above. Key cross-reference: pre-training did not show expertise reversal, suggesting activation is distinct from scaffolding.

### Implications for Schema Theory in the Age of AI
- Synthesis point from across papers: AI cognitive offloading bypasses the schema-construction process because students skip the effortful encoding that creates schemas. Prior-knowledge-activation routines become a countermeasure to AI-induced schema thinness.

## 7. Interleaving vs Blocked Practice - Recent Nuances

### Undesirable Difficulty of Interleaved Practice: The Importance of Initial Blocked Practice for Low-Achieving Adolescents (Hwang, 2025)
- **Venue**: Language Learning (Wiley)
- **URL**: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12659
- **Key findings**: Low-achieving adolescents showed *harmful* effects from pure interleaving. A **hybrid group** (blocked first, interleaved later) showed the most robust retention at 2-week delayed test; the pure-interleaving group was more prone to forgetting.
- **Pedagogical implication**: This is the most pedagogically actionable 2025 finding on interleaving. For mixed-ability gymnasium classes, use **blocked-then-interleaved sequences** rather than pure interleaving. Establish the schema first, then force discrimination.

### Whether Interleaving or Blocking Is More Effective Depends on Learning Strategy (2025)
- **Venue**: Behavioral Sciences, 15(5), 662
- **URL**: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12108632/
- **Key findings**: For **memorisation** (similarity-based classification): interleaving wins. For **rule-finding** (rule-based classification): blocking wins. The learning strategy of the student, not just the material, determines optimality.
- **Pedagogical implication**: In historia, memorising cases (specific revolutions, war years) benefits from interleaving. Teaching a causal model (why revolutions occur) benefits from blocking.

### Tailoring Interleaved Practice: Does Adaptive Sequencing Boost the Interleaving Effect? (2025)
- **Venue**: Learning and Instruction
- **URL**: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1041608025001803
- **Key findings**: Adaptive sequencing (algorithmically choosing next item based on recent performance) boosts the interleaving benefit versus fixed sequencing. The mechanism: discrimination difficulty is highest when items are confusable given current skill level.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Spaced-repetition tools like Anki with ease-factor adjustment already implement this. Fixed worksheets do not.

### Why Do Learners (Under)Utilize Interleaving? (Yan et al., 2024)
- **Venue**: Educational Psychology Review
- **URL**: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-024-09902-0
- **Key findings**: Students know interleaving is effective but still prefer blocking because they underestimate the **utility value of distinguishing** categories and because blocking feels more fluent.
- **Pedagogical implication**: Teachers must explicitly explain *why* mixing is happening and show performance evidence; otherwise students revert to blocked self-study.

## 8. Handwriting vs Typing

### Handwriting but Not Typewriting Leads to Widespread Brain Connectivity: A High-Density EEG Study (Van der Meer & van der Weel, 2024)
- **Venue**: Frontiers in Psychology (republished 2024)
- **URL**: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945/full
- **Sample**: 36 university students, 256-sensor high-density EEG
- **Key findings**: Handwriting activated significantly more widespread and interconnected brain networks than typing, specifically in **theta (3.5-7.5 Hz)** and **alpha (8-12.5 Hz)** bands - frequency bands associated with memory formation and attention. Connectivity of brain regions increased during handwriting but not typing.
- **Pedagogical implication**: For encoding new vocabulary, terminology, or key concepts (definitions in samhällskunskap), handwritten notes are neurocognitively superior. For composing long essays where fluency matters more than encoding, typing is acceptable.

### Commentary on Van der Meer: Methodological Limitations (Marquardt et al., 2025)
- **Venue**: Frontiers in Psychology
- **URL**: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1517235/full
- **Key findings**: The commentary notes that Van der Meer's participants repeatedly wrote well-known words without any memory-encoding requirement, so the study does not directly prove a learning advantage - only a connectivity difference. Calls for replications with memory outcomes.
- **Pedagogical implication**: The connectivity finding is real; the learning implication remains partly inferential. Combined with Mueller & Oppenheimer's original behavioural work (2014) and Kosmyna's 2025 "cognitive debt" findings on typing + AI, the overall evidence still favours handwriting for encoding.

### The Neuroscience Behind Writing: Handwriting vs. Typing (2025 review)
- **URL**: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389277851_The_Neuroscience_Behind_Writing_Handwriting_vs_Typing-Who_Wins_the_Battle
- **Key findings**: Review consolidates the fine-motor hypothesis (precise hand movements generate richer sensorimotor feedback), the generation-effect hypothesis (slower writing forces summarising), and the visuospatial hypothesis (handwritten notes have individual layout features aiding retrieval). All three likely contribute.
- **Pedagogical implication**: The three mechanisms are complementary; handwriting wins on multiple fronts, not just one.

## Cross-Cutting Themes

1. **"Short-term performance vs. long-term learning" divergence**: GenAI, typing, blocked practice, and unscaffolded instruction all produce good immediate results but worse delayed/transfer outcomes. This is the classic desirable-difficulty pattern, now extended into the digital/AI era. Assessment that measures only immediate performance systematically rewards the worst long-term strategies.

2. **Element interactivity as the unifying construct**: The 2024-2025 CLT literature increasingly reduces multiple CLT effects (expertise reversal, worked example, split attention) to a single underlying mechanism - how many elements the working-memory system must juggle simultaneously. This simplifies instructional decisions: always ask "can I reduce element interactivity for novices?"

3. **The metacognitive-offloading hypothesis**: Across AI, phone-presence, and digital-reading research, the most damaging mechanism is not the tool itself but the *delegation of metacognitive regulation* to the environment. Phones do not just distract - they disable the self-monitoring that catches mind-wandering. AI does not just answer questions - it disables the evaluation/verification cycle.

4. **Differential effects favor disadvantaged students**: Phone bans (Norway), worked examples (CLT), prior-knowledge activation, and hybrid interleaving all show **larger effects for lower-SES / lower-achieving students**. These are equity interventions, not just pedagogy optimisations.

5. **The "schema first, discrimination later" meta-pattern**: Blocked-then-interleaved, worked-example-then-problem-solving, handwriting-then-typing all share a common shape: build a schema under low-interactivity conditions, then introduce desirable difficulties that force discrimination and transfer. This is not new theoretically but the 2024-2025 evidence specifies the sequence much more precisely.

## Contrarian / Counterintuitive Findings

1. **Working memory training is dead**: The most-hyped cognitive intervention of 2010-2020 has essentially zero evidence for far-transfer to academic outcomes. Classroom investment is misallocated there. (Teixeira-Santos et al., 2024)

2. **AI raises essay grades but not learning**: The Fan et al. (2025) metacognitive-laziness study shows ChatGPT-assisted essays score highest, yet knowledge transfer is indistinguishable from controls. Grading policies that reward surface-quality essays now actively reward non-learning.

3. **Pre-training escapes the expertise-reversal trap**: Unlike other forms of instructional support, pre-training reduced extraneous load across prior-knowledge levels. A rare "low-cost, no-downside" technique (Frontiers in Psychology, 2025).

4. **Mere smartphone presence, not use, impairs cognition**: Even silent phones in pockets produce measurable attention costs in meta-analyses (g=-0.14). Silent-mode compromise policies capture less than half of the available benefit of full-day bans.

5. **Interleaving can harm low-achieving adolescents**: The Hwang (2025) study directly challenges the blanket "interleaving is better" claim that has spread through popular pedagogy. For struggling gymnasium students, pure interleaving impaired retention vs. a hybrid blocked-then-interleaved design.

6. **The "cognitive debt cross-over"**: In Kosmyna et al., students who used ChatGPT for three sessions and then were told to write without it in Session 4 *still* showed reduced brain connectivity. AI dependence produces persistent cognitive deficits even after tool removal - analogous to training debt, not just situational offloading.

7. **Brain-drain replication crisis alongside robust meta-analytic signal**: Ward et al. (2017) individual-study failures to replicate coexist with a robust small-to-moderate meta-analytic effect. Paradox resolved: the effect is real but smaller than originally claimed and highly sensitive to task design.

## Implications for Swedish Gymnasium Teaching (Social Studies / History)

### Concrete classroom protocols derived from 2024-2026 research

1. **Phone policy**: Support full-day bans (phones in lockers/bags, not pockets). Abrahamsson (2024) evidence is strong, and mere-presence meta-analytic effects confirm partial restriction is partial benefit. Particularly important for equity - lower-SES girls benefit most.

2. **AI use sequencing**: First draft without AI, revision with AI, final polish independent again. Grade the process (draft sequences, think-aloud, oral defense) not only the final product to counter "metacognitive laziness". For source analysis (källkritik), require manual annotation before AI consultation.

3. **Handwriting for encoding**: In historia specifically, require handwritten timelines, concept definitions, and key-term glossaries. For longer analytical essays, typing is acceptable. The split is by learning function, not by preference.

4. **Lesson opener (2-3 min)**: Retrieval-based prior-knowledge activation - students list what they already know on the day's topic before new content. Zero downside (pre-training does not trigger expertise reversal) and particularly beneficial for low-prior-knowledge students.

5. **Worked-example fading**: First example of a new concept type (e.g., "analyze this ideology") fully worked on board. Second: partial. Third: students independent. Calibrate by observed performance, not lesson number - the expertise reversal point varies by student.

6. **Blocked-then-interleaved sequences**: For new concepts in samhällskunskap, spend one lesson on the new concept in isolation (blocked), then interleave with prior concepts in subsequent practice (Hwang 2025; Nakata & Suzuki). Do not start with interleaved practice for mixed-ability classes.

7. **Coherence in slides**: Every decorative image costs learning. Use Mayer's coherence principle as a ruthless edit: if the image does not carry content, remove it. Especially relevant for AI-generated images - ease of creation breeds coherence violations.

8. **Exit tickets and retrieval-based closures**: Already well-covered in existing SH1A1 material. Cross-reference: combine with prior-knowledge activation opener for a bookended lesson design that activates and consolidates schemas.

9. **Reading long historical sources**: Paper or e-ink preferred over tablets/laptops when comprehension of expository text longer than ~1000 words is the target. The digital-reading meta-analytic penalty is small per-source but compounds.

10. **Assessment design**: Prefer oral defenses, handwritten in-class assessments, and process portfolios for work where AI temptation is high. Use AI-accessible assessment only when the learning target genuinely involves AI-collaboration skills.
