# Lesson Planning Frameworks and Instructional Design Models - Research Report

**Date**: 2026-03-07
**Prepared by**: Research Specialist Agent (Cornelius v03.26)
**Target Audience**: Swedish upper secondary teacher (samhällskunskap/historia) using Claude Code for lesson planning

---

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes the most current research (2020-2025) on lesson planning frameworks, instructional design models, and evidence-based lesson structuring for upper secondary education. The central finding is that **no single framework dominates all contexts**, but a convergence of evidence points toward a hybrid model that integrates: (1) backward design logic at the unit level, (2) Rosenshine-based explicit instruction structure at the lesson level, and (3) inquiry and discussion cycles embedded within that structure [1][2][3].

The teacher addressed here already possesses strong pedagogical knowledge in retrieval practice, formative assessment, discussion techniques, and questioning. The critical gap research identifies is not individual strategy knowledge but **coherent lesson architecture** - how these strategies fit together in sequence within a bounded lesson, and how individual lessons nest within broader unit design [4][5].

Swedish didaktik tradition, particularly through Skolverket's structured instruction model and Skolforskningsinstitutet's subject-specific systematic reviews, offers an important complementary lens to Anglo-Saxon instructional design. The Swedish model emphasizes the teacher's analytical judgment (didaktisk analys) before planning, not just the mechanical application of lesson templates [6][7].

Emerging research on AI-assisted lesson planning (2024-2025) finds that AI tools significantly reduce planning time but systematically underperform on higher-order thinking, multicultural perspectives, and differentiation - precisely the dimensions most valued in history and civics teaching [8][9].

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: The Empirical Hierarchy of Lesson Planning Frameworks

Not all frameworks are equal. Recent meta-analyses and systematic reviews allow a rough empirical ranking:

**Tier 1 - Strong Evidence Base:**

**Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction** (Rosenshine, 2012; Sherrington, 2019 application)
The principles derive from three converging lines of evidence: cognitive science research on memory and learning, studies of master teachers whose students showed highest achievement gains, and research on cognitive supports. The 10 principles have been translated into a practical lesson structure by Tom Sherrington [2][3]. Research-based effect sizes are embedded throughout the principles (e.g., the recommendation that effective teachers spend 57% of lesson time on guided practice reflects Rosenshine's original observation studies).

Key lesson structure implications:
- 5-8 minute daily review at lesson start
- New material in small steps with worked examples
- High-frequency questioning to all students (not just volunteers)
- Guided practice before independent practice
- Weekly and monthly cumulative review

**Skolverket's Strukturerad Undervisning (Structured Teaching) - 6-step model**
This is the Swedish national adaptation of direct instruction principles, explicitly based on Rosenshine's research, and represents Skolverket's clearest structural recommendation for lesson design [6]. The six steps are: (1) Introduction/motivation, (2) Teacher presentation in small steps with examples and checking questions, (3) Guided practice with teacher and peer support, (4) Summary and learning check, (5) Independent practice, (6) Evaluation and assessment. The model has "stark positiv påverkan på elevernas skolprestationer" (strong positive impact on student academic performance) according to the supporting research base.

**Tier 2 - Strong Evidence, Different Level of Analysis:**

**Understanding by Design / Backward Design (Wiggins & McTighe, 1998/2005)**
Backward design operates primarily at the **unit and course level**, not the individual lesson level. Starting from desired enduring understandings and assessment evidence before designing instruction is consistently recommended in medical education (2024 rapid review), teacher education programs, and secondary curriculum design [10][11]. A 2023 study found that backward design successfully increased active learning integration when applied to undergraduate course redesign. The framework's strength is alignment: ensuring daily lessons serve long-term transfer goals. Its limitation is that it does not specify lesson-level microstructure - it tells you *what* to teach, not *how* to sequence learning within a lesson.

**5E Instructional Model (Bybee et al.)**
A landmark 2024 meta-analysis by Polanin and colleagues (61 RCT studies, 156 effect sizes) found the 5E model produced effect size g = 0.82 for science outcomes, g = 0.73 for mathematics [12]. This represents a 29-percentile-rank improvement for science and 26 for math compared to control groups. However, critically: 5E is best suited for **multi-lesson units** (2-3 weeks) rather than individual lessons, and is most appropriate when students encounter genuinely new concepts requiring inquiry and exploration. The model has strongest evidence in STEM; evidence in history and civics is thinner.

**Tier 3 - Practitioner Frameworks with Emerging Evidence:**

**Teaching WalkThrus (Sherrington & Caviglioli, 2020-2023)**
Three volumes of five-step visual instructional coaching guides covering 150+ teaching techniques across domains: curriculum planning, explaining and modelling, questioning and feedback, practice and retrieval, and classroom culture [13]. No single meta-analysis covers WalkThrus, but each technique is grounded in research (Rosenshine, cognitive load, retrieval practice). The framework's value is not as a lesson plan template but as a **vocabulary for instructional decisions** within lessons.

**Teach Like a Champion 3.0 (Lemov, 2021)**
A revised practitioner framework based on systematic video analysis of high-performing teachers. The 2021 edition added a chapter specifically on **lesson preparation** - how to adapt a generic lesson plan for a specific class, including planning for exemplars, anticipated errors, variations, student action, and knowledge organizer use [14]. Strongest evidence base comes from specific techniques (cold calling, no-opt-out, etc.) rather than the overall framework.

### Finding 2: Backward Design vs. Forward Design - What Research Actually Shows

The research question of "which is better" is somewhat misleading because they operate at different levels [10][15]:

- **Backward design** (UbD): Curriculum and unit level. Ensures coherence between goals, assessments, and learning activities. Prevents "coverage-focused" planning where activities are selected without clear purpose. Research support is strongest in higher education and medical education contexts.

- **Forward design** (traditional lesson planning): Can produce effective lessons if teachers have deep subject knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge, but research shows a tendency toward content coverage rather than transfer and understanding.

- **The synthesis**: Current evidence supports using **backward design logic for unit planning** (4-6 week sequences) combined with **Rosenshine/direct instruction principles for individual lesson microstructure**. These are not competing frameworks - they answer different questions.

A 2024 German study on lesson planning complexity (Kueth & Scholl, Journal for Adult Education) found that the lesson planning task is genuinely complex because teachers must simultaneously manage: content selection, sequencing decisions, time allocation, anticipated student responses, differentiation, and assessment integration [4]. This complexity explains why experienced teachers benefit most from having multiple frameworks available as heuristics rather than rigid templates.

### Finding 3: Learning Intentions and Success Criteria - Research Status

A body of evidence supports the explicit use of learning intentions (learning objectives shared with students) and success criteria (observable indicators of achievement). Key findings:

- Hattie and Donoghue's meta-analysis found success criteria have a potential effect size of .88 - substantially above the average effect of .40 [15]
- Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO) research supports clear learning goals as "one of the most impactful ways to enhance student engagement and achievement" [15]
- Pearson Schools research (2024) recommends that learning intentions be referenced **throughout** the lesson, not only shared at the beginning [15]
- Critical nuance: learning intentions and success criteria work best when they are **co-constructed** with students or at minimum explained in terms students can use for self-regulation - not simply posted on a board

**For samhällskunskap and historia specifically**: Research on history education (Skolforskningsinstitutet systematic review) emphasizes that historical understanding is not about factual recall but about developing analytical capacity and the ability to "förhålla sig till och analysera" (engage with and analyze) historical knowledge [7]. This means learning intentions in history lessons should reference **cognitive processes** (analyzing, comparing perspectives, evaluating evidence) not just content knowledge. Success criteria for a history lesson on World War I causes would be more effective as "you can explain how multiple factors interacted" rather than "you know three causes of WWI."

### Finding 4: Integrating Retrieval Practice, Formative Assessment, and Questioning into Lesson Structure

This is the central synthesis question for the target audience. Research increasingly shows these are not separate strategies but components of a unified lesson architecture [1][16]:

**The integrated lesson structure emerging from research:**

**Phase 1: Retrieval Review (5-10 min)**
Begin with retrieval practice on previous learning, not re-teaching. This is Rosenshine's first principle ("begin with a short review of previous learning") but grounded in memory science - retrieval strengthens storage and identifies gaps. Exit ticket data from the previous lesson can inform what to review. In history/civics, this might be: low-stakes quiz on previous content, vocabulary retrieval, timeline sequencing, or "what connections can you make from last lesson to what we're doing today?"

**Phase 2: Learning Intentions and Activation (2-5 min)**
Share the learning intention (in student-friendly language) and activate prior knowledge or create cognitive hook (Skolverket's Step 1 - "Introduction/motivation"). For history, this might be a provocative primary source excerpt, a contemporary connection, or a "what do you think and why?" question.

**Phase 3: Explicit Instruction with Worked Examples (10-15 min)**
New content in small steps, teacher-modelled with think-alouds, checking for understanding through questions to all students (cold call or random sampling). Rosenshine's Principle 2-4. Cognitive load theory implies: manage intrinsic load by sequencing from simple to complex, and minimize extraneous load (don't overload slides with text). For history/civics: present source material, model the analytical process, then students practice.

**Phase 4: Guided Practice with Formative Assessment (15-20 min)**
The "We Do" phase. Students work with teacher circulating, asking checking questions, providing targeted feedback. Dylan Wiliam's formative assessment strategies fit here: all-student response techniques (mini-whiteboards, voting, paired response), clarifying questions, peer feedback. This is the longest phase - Rosenshine found effective teachers spend 57% of lesson time on guided practice.

**Phase 5: Independent Practice or Discussion (10-15 min)**
"You Do" - students demonstrate learning independently. For history/civics, this might be written argumentation, document analysis, structured discussion, or source evaluation. This phase generates data for the teacher's next lesson plan.

**Phase 6: Lesson Close - Summary and Metacognition (3-5 min)**
Exit ticket, pair-share summary, or "muddiest point" card. Explicitly connect back to learning intention. Skolverket's model calls this the learning check before independent practice, but research also supports ending the lesson with a retrieval or synthesis activity.

**Total: 50-60 minute lesson structure with evidence-based phases**

### Finding 5: The Planning-Teaching Gap

Research consistently shows that good lesson plans do not automatically produce good lessons [4][17]. The implementation gap is a real phenomenon:

- When programs implemented with fidelity are compared to programs not implemented with fidelity, fidelity-implemented programs produce average effect sizes **2-3 times higher** [17]
- 70-80% of educational change efforts fail, largely due to implementation gaps
- Teacher experience matters: programs researched using master teachers produce weaker results when implemented by novice teachers

**Key mechanisms of the planning-teaching gap in practice:**

1. **Planned time vs. enacted time**: Teachers consistently underestimate how long activities take, particularly discussions and complex tasks. Research on lesson pacing (ASCD) recommends planning for "engaged time" (students cognitively active) as the key variable, not clock time [18]

2. **In-lesson decision-making overrides plans**: Teachers face thousands of micro-decisions during lessons. Without well-internalized frameworks and routines, these decisions are reactive rather than principled

3. **Differentiation collapses under real-time pressure**: Plans that include differentiated pathways are often abandoned when lessons don't go as expected

4. **Classroom management interrupts planned structure**: Managing behavior disrupts the sequential phases of lesson plans

**Practical implications**: Research supports **lesson plan templates with built-in flexibility** and **contingency planning** (what will I do if students haven't understood Step X?) rather than overly rigid scripts. Lemov's lesson preparation approach explicitly includes "planning for errors" - anticipating student misconceptions before the lesson [14].

### Finding 6: Cognitive Load Theory and Lesson Sequencing

Recent CLT research (2020-2025) has refined its implications for lesson planning [5]:

**Three types of load and their planning implications:**

- **Intrinsic load** (complexity of the material itself): Managed by sequencing from simple to complex, teaching component skills before combining them, and building prior knowledge. For history: teach the French Revolution as a sequence of phases before students analyze the complexity of overlapping causes.

- **Extraneous load** (unnecessary cognitive load from poor presentation): Managed through clear explanations, well-designed materials, avoiding split attention (when text and diagram are spatially separated). Mayer's multimedia principles (which the teacher already knows) apply here.

- **Germane load** (productive cognitive effort leading to schema formation): Encouraged through self-explanation, elaboration, and active retrieval. This is the load teachers should **maximize** within the available capacity.

**Contemporary Task Sequencing Research (2024)**:
A 2024 study found that **whole-part sequencing with simple backward chaining** was the most effective approach - teach the overall picture, then break into components, then reassemble [5]. For a samhällskunskap lesson on democratic systems: show the whole system first (overview), then analyze components (parliament, judiciary, media), then reconstruct relationships.

**A recent MDPI review (2025)** on emerging CLT trends found a shift toward intervention research and away from purely experimental studies, with growing interest in how CLT applies to online and technology-enhanced learning environments.

### Finding 7: Differentiation in Lesson Planning

Research on Universal Design for Learning (UDL 3.0, released July 2024) and Differentiated Instruction offers a nuanced picture [19][20]:

- **UDL** is proactive: design lessons from the start with multiple means of representation, expression, and engagement, anticipating diverse needs
- **Differentiated Instruction (DI)** is responsive: adjust during instruction based on ongoing assessment of student needs
- Recent research (Gheyssens, Griful-Freixenet & Struyven, 2024) reframes DI as a **teaching mindset** rather than a toolkit, emphasizing growth mindset and flexible grouping

**Planning-level differentiation strategies with research support:**
- Tiered tasks (same learning intention, different complexity)
- Flexible questioning sequences (scaffold with simpler versions before complex versions)
- Multiple entry points for primary sources (visual, textual, audio)
- Choice boards for independent practice
- Pre-assessment to adjust instruction rather than assuming what students know

**For gymnasium (upper secondary) history and civics**: Differentiation at this level often focuses less on ability differences and more on: different background knowledge, different linguistic capacity (particularly for L2 students), and different prior engagement with controversial topics. UDL's emphasis on multiple means of representation is particularly relevant for source-heavy history lessons.

### Finding 8: Lesson Study as Planning-as-Professional-Development

Lesson study (jugyou kenkyuu) is a collaborative planning and reflection model that has shown impact in Japan for over 100 years and is expanding internationally [21][22]:

**The lesson study cycle:**
1. Collaborative research lesson planning (focused on a research question about student learning)
2. One teacher delivers the lesson while colleagues observe (not the teacher but student learning)
3. Post-lesson discussion focused on evidence from student responses
4. Revision of lesson based on observations
5. Optional: reteach revised lesson

**Research findings on impact:**
- 2024 research (General Didactics meets Subject Didactics, RISTAL journal) found lesson study creates productive intersection between general pedagogy and subject didactics [21]
- Key finding: lesson study improves not individual lessons but **teacher thinking about lesson design** - it builds planning competence over time
- Collaborative Lesson Research (CLR) model (Takahashi, Lewis) identifies structural features that maximize impact outside Japan: clear research question, knowledge-based planning, public research lesson, knowledgeable outside commentator

**For Swedish school context**: Lesson study aligns well with Skolverket's emphasis on kollegialt lärande (collegial learning) and the VAK (Vidareutveckling och analys av kvalitet i undervisning) professional development framework. It represents a systematic way to develop lesson planning competence that goes beyond individual teacher reflection.

### Finding 9: AI-Assisted Lesson Planning - Current Research (2024-2025)

This is the most rapidly developing area. Research findings are mixed but converging [8][9][23][24]:

**What AI tools do well:**
- Generate initial lesson plan structures quickly
- Suggest activity ideas and resources
- Create differentiated versions of the same material
- Reduce administrative planning burden (50% of teachers report using AI for lesson planning in 2024-2025)
- AI feedback on lesson plans can match or surpass human feedback in certain dimensions: content accuracy, lesson structure, and learner engagement ideas

**Critical weaknesses (relevant to history/civics specifically):**

A 2025 analysis of 310 AI-generated civics lesson plans (created by ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot) found [8]:
- 90% of activities focused on **lower-order thinking** (remember, understand) - dominated by recall and comprehension
- Only 10% promoted higher-order thinking (analyze, evaluate, create)
- Over 94% of activities lacked diverse perspectives
- Plans emphasized patriotic symbols while ignoring experiences of marginalized groups
- "Nearly half of all activities were labeled as 'remember'" - passive learning rather than civic engagement

A 2024 vocational teacher education study (longitudinal, n=103) found AI feedback on lesson plans improved:
- Early phases of action-oriented instruction (inform, plan, decide)
- Lesson structure clarity
But AI feedback was less effective for: adapting to specific student contexts, culturally responsive content, and complex pedagogical reasoning [23].

**Clark & van Kessel (2024)** evaluated ChatGPT, Bing, and Bard generating social studies lesson plans and found AI could produce structurally coherent plans but struggled with: accuracy of historical content, including nuanced perspectives, and creating inquiry-based activities that go beyond information retrieval [9].

**Practical recommendation from research**: Use AI as a **first-draft generator**, then apply subject expertise and pedagogical judgment to:
1. Elevate cognitive demand (move from "describe" to "evaluate," "analyze," "construct argument")
2. Add primary sources and authentic materials
3. Integrate discussion and deliberation activities
4. Ensure multiple historical perspectives are represented
5. Connect explicitly to current relevance (essential for samhällskunskap)

The CIVIC framework (2025, CITE Journal) offers five principles for AI use in social studies: Critical engagement, Inquiry integration, Varied perspectives, Informed citizenship, and Contextual adaptation [8].

### Finding 10: Swedish Didaktik and the Didaktisk Triangel

The Swedish/Scandinavian didaktik tradition offers a conceptual framework that operates at a different level than Anglo-Saxon instructional design [6][7][25]:

**The Didaktisk Triangel (Didactic Triangle):**
Three relational axes between: Teacher - Student - Content (subject matter). Effective teaching requires conscious attention to all three relationships, not just content delivery. The teacher-student relationship (classroom culture, trust, authority) shapes what content can be taught and how. The student-content relationship (prior knowledge, interest, relevance) shapes what teaching can accomplish.

**Klafki's Didaktische Analyse (Didactic Analysis):**
Wolfgang Klafki's framework asks teachers to analyze content through several lenses before planning:
- **Gegenwartsbedeutung**: What is the content's current significance for students' lives?
- **Zukunftsbedeutung**: What future significance will this content have?
- **Exemplarische Bedeutung**: What general principle does this content exemplify?
- **Thematische Struktur**: What is the essential structure of the content?
- **Zugänglichkeit**: How is this content made accessible?

This pre-planning analytical process is what distinguishes the didaktik tradition from the Anglo-Saxon model. In the Swedish tradition, **lesson planning begins with content analysis**, not activity design. This is a crucial complement to Rosenshine and backward design, both of which emphasize learning sequence and pedagogy but say less about the teacher's interpretive relationship with the subject matter.

**Skolverket's Practical Adaptation:**
Skolverket's lesson planning guidance starts from the curriculum (kursplanens syfte och centrala innehåll) and asks teachers to formulate lesson goals (lektionsmål) from these curricular purposes. The six-step structured teaching model then provides the pedagogical architecture within which content is delivered [6]. The lektionsplaneringsprocess thus involves: curricular analysis → learning goals → assessment design → lesson structure → activity selection.

## Synthesis: What Does an Evidence-Based Lesson Plan Look Like?

Drawing all findings together, an evidence-based lesson plan for a Swedish upper secondary history or civics teacher should integrate the following elements:

### Pre-Planning Phase (The Didaktisk Analys):

Before writing the lesson plan, engage with:
- **Curricular anchor**: Which competency/content from kursplanen does this lesson address?
- **Transfer goal**: What do students need to be able to do with this knowledge beyond this lesson? (Backward design question)
- **Content analysis**: What is the essential structure of this content? What are the key concepts, perspectives, and tensions? (Klafki-inspired)
- **Prior knowledge assessment**: What do students already know? What misconceptions are common? (Informs Step 1 and differentiation)
- **Learning intention**: One clear statement of what students will be able to do by end of lesson (verb matters: analyze, evaluate, compare - not just "know" or "understand")
- **Success criteria**: 2-3 observable indicators that the learning intention was met

### Lesson Architecture (Rosenshine + Strukturerad Undervisning):

**Opening (8-12 min)**
- Retrieval review: 5-8 min on previous lesson content (quiz, pair retrieval, exit ticket follow-up)
- Share learning intention in student language and connect to broader unit/course goals
- Hook/cognitive activation: primary source, contemporary connection, provocative question

**Explicit Instruction (10-15 min)**
- New content in 2-3 small steps, with worked examples
- Teacher models analytical process with think-aloud (not just content delivery)
- Checking questions to all students after each step (cold call, hands-up-alternatives)
- Targeted formative mini-assessment before moving forward

**Guided Practice (15-20 min)**
- Students practice with support (pairs, small group, teacher circulating)
- Progressively release responsibility (from paired to individual)
- Teacher provides targeted feedback on specific errors/misconceptions
- Structured discussion or deliberation activity fits naturally here for history/civics

**Independent Practice or Application (10-15 min)**
- Students demonstrate learning independently (written argument, source analysis, structured task)
- Appropriate level of challenge: "Difficult enough to be challenging but solvable for most"
- Teacher observes for formative data to inform next lesson planning

**Lesson Close (3-5 min)**
- Exit ticket or retrieval activity to assess learning intention achievement
- Student summary ("pair-share: tell your partner the three most important things from today")
- Preview of next lesson (supports encoding and anticipation)

### Planning Documentation Should Include:
- Learning intention and success criteria
- Contingency planning: "If students haven't understood X, I will..."
- Differentiation notes: at minimum, lower/higher entry points
- Formative assessment moments: what, when, and what I'll do with data
- Key questions (written in advance, not improvised)
- Time allocation for each phase
- Resources and materials
- Connection to previous and next lessons (horizontal alignment)
- Connection to unit transfer goal (vertical alignment / backward design)

## Comparison of Major Frameworks

| Framework | Primary Level | Strongest Evidence | Best For | Key Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rosenshine's Principles | Lesson (micro) | Cognitive science + teacher effectiveness research | Structured explicit instruction | Less guidance on inquiry/discussion phases |
| Backward Design (UbD) | Unit/Course | Multiple contexts | Alignment between goals, assessment, activities | Does not specify lesson-level structure |
| 5E Model | Unit (2-3 weeks) | g=0.82 science meta-analysis (2024) | Inquiry-based learning new concepts | STEM-focused; weaker evidence in humanities |
| Strukturerad undervisning (Skolverket) | Lesson (macro) | Based on Rosenshine | Swedish gymnasium context | Six steps relatively rigid for discussion-heavy lessons |
| Didaktisk analys (Klafki) | Pre-planning | Theoretical tradition | Content analysis and subject significance | Less operational - analytical tool not lesson template |
| Teaching WalkThrus (Sherrington) | Technique level | Grounded in research | Professional development vocabulary | Not a lesson plan framework |
| Lesson Study (Jugyou Kenkyuu) | Collaborative planning | Japanese system + international adaptation | Building planning competence over time | Time-intensive; requires collegial infrastructure |
| UDL 3.0 (CAST, 2024) | Design level | Systematic reviews | Inclusive lesson design | Implementation complexity |

## Market/Industry Context: Lesson Planning Research Trends

**Trend 1: From Lesson Plans as Documents to Planning as Thinking**
Research is increasingly distinguishing between the lesson plan artifact (the document) and planning competence (the teacher's ability to make good instructional decisions). A 2024 scoping review of science teachers' lesson planning competence (Grossmann et al., Studies in Science Education) found that most research emphasizes **knowledge** components of planning over **procedural** skills - what teachers know vs. what they can do. The field is moving toward treating planning as a complex cognitive skill that develops through practice and feedback [4].

**Trend 2: Lesson Plan Quality Assessment Instruments**
The RALP (Rubric to Assess Lesson Plans, Grossmann & Kruger, 2024) is a new empirically validated instrument for assessing lesson plan quality based on the Pentagon Model of Pedagogical Content Knowledge. This represents growing research interest in measuring planning quality itself, not just lesson outcomes [4].

**Trend 3: Technology Integration in Planning**
A 2026 study (Teaching and Teacher Education) on technology integration in student teachers' lesson plans found that planned technology integration is associated with specific dimensions of instructional quality but does not automatically improve it. The TPTI model (Technology exploitation + Instructional quality) is emerging as a conceptual framework [4].

**Trend 4: Collaborative and AI-Augmented Planning**
Both lesson study and AI tools represent a shift from solo teacher planning toward collaborative or augmented planning. A 2025 Springer study on shared collaborative lesson planning (SCLP) found that CSCL tools enabled real-time feedback and resource sharing, improving plan quality compared to individual planning [22].

## Opportunities for the Swedish History/Civics Teacher

1. **Integrate didaktisk analys as a pre-planning ritual** - before creating the lesson template, spend 5-10 minutes on Klafki-inspired questions about content significance. This is what distinguishes an expert lesson plan from a competent one.

2. **Apply backward design at the unit level (4-6 weeks), Rosenshine at the lesson level** - these frameworks answer different questions and can be used simultaneously.

3. **Use the integrated 6-phase lesson structure** as a flexible template, not a rigid script. The structure creates predictability for students (reducing cognitive load on "what are we doing") while allowing flexibility in content and activities within phases.

4. **Leverage AI tools for Phase 3 material generation** (worked examples, source selection, differentiated texts) while writing Phase 1 (retrieval review), key questions, and success criteria yourself - the dimensions where AI underperforms.

5. **For samhällskunskap specifically**: The civic education research emphasizes that AI-generated civics plans overwhelmingly focus on passive recall. Design the Guided Practice and Discussion phases yourself, ensuring deliberative discussion (deliberative democracy education) and higher-order thinking tasks.

6. **For historia specifically**: Skolforskningsinstitutet's research emphasizes classroom interaction and dialogue around sources as central to developing historical understanding. This maps to Phase 4 (guided practice with discussion). Design these phases with explicit attention to the "orchestrating instruction" dimension the review identified.

7. **Establish a simple exit ticket protocol** across all lessons. The data gathered feeds directly into the next lesson's retrieval review (Phase 1), creating a planning-teaching-feedback loop that improves both planning and learning over time.

## Challenges and Risks

**The Template Trap**: Research shows that over-reliance on lesson plan templates can paradoxically reduce lesson quality by encouraging mechanical compliance over adaptive teaching. The six-phase structure above is a heuristic, not a checklist.

**The AI Quality Gap**: AI-generated lesson plans for history and civics systematically underperform on the dimensions that matter most: higher-order thinking, multicultural perspectives, and critical inquiry. Uncritical adoption of AI plans risks lowering lesson quality.

**The Planning-Teaching Gap**: Even excellent lesson plans do not guarantee excellent lessons. Research emphasizes that lesson plan quality and implementation quality are semi-independent - experienced teachers benefit from building flexible routines and contingency planning into their plans.

**Differentiation Complexity**: Current research on UDL and differentiated instruction identifies implementation as genuinely difficult. At gymnasium level, differentiation often collapses under real-time lesson pressure. Planning specific differentiated entry points (not just "extension tasks") requires deliberate attention.

## Research Notes and Caveats

1. **Evidence asymmetry by subject**: Most empirical research on lesson planning frameworks uses science and mathematics as contexts. Evidence directly from historia or samhällskunskap at gymnasium level is thinner, though the Swedish systematic reviews from Skolforskningsinstitutet fill some of this gap.

2. **5E limitations for humanities**: The 5E model's large effect sizes come from STEM contexts. Its applicability to history and civics is theoretically plausible (inquiry cycle fits historical inquiry) but less empirically validated. The model's "Exploration before Explanation" sequence may not fit all lesson types in humanities.

3. **Rosenshine's observational basis**: Rosenshine's original research was largely observational (master teacher studies) and correlational. The principles are strongly supported by cognitive science but the original effect sizes are not from RCTs. This does not undermine the framework but should be understood as its evidentiary basis.

4. **AI research is moving rapidly**: The 2024-2025 findings on AI lesson planning tools will likely be outdated within 12-18 months as model capabilities improve. The structural weaknesses identified (higher-order thinking, multicultural perspectives) may be addressed in future model versions.

5. **Lgy25 curriculum change**: Sweden's new curriculum (Lgy25) applies from July 2025. Lesson planning frameworks need to be applied to new kursplaner for gymnasiet. The core didaktik principles remain valid but specific curricular anchors for historia and samhällskunskap have changed.

6. **Further investigation recommended**:
   - Skolforskningsinstitutet's full systematic review on kritiskt tänkande och källkritik i samhällskunskap (critical thinking and source criticism in civics)
   - EEF's updated guidance reports (2024) on specific instructional strategies for secondary schools
   - Research on lesson planning for Swedish-as-second-language contexts in gymnasium (relevant given student demographics)
